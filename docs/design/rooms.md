## 1. The house

Four pressure rooms and a Bathroom, connected by impossible Penrose stairs **[Decided]**. Each room is one source of appearance pressure from body-image research (the **Tripartite Influence Model**: family, peers, media; Thompson et al., 1999), plus significant others (SATAQ-4R; Schaefer et al., 2017) and the workplace. See the [quiz design](act0-quiz.md) §1 for the research and Korean context behind each one.

| Room | Source of pressure | What it wants from the protagonist | Mask it builds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Family** | Parents, siblings, extended family | To be a son or daughter they can be proud of | **The Perfect Child**: obedient, grateful, presentable [Decided] |
| **Friends & Partner** | Friends and a significant other | To fit in, and to be wanted | **The Easy One**: self-deprecating, non-threatening, always fine [Decided] |
| **Society** | Coworkers, a boss, interviewers | To be employable and "professional" | **The Presentable One**: polished, agreeable, low-maintenance [Proposed] |
| **Social Media** | Followers, strangers, the algorithm | To be seen, liked, and better than yesterday | **The Filtered Self**: aspirational, flawless, always "on" [Decided] |
| **Bathroom** | The protagonist's own gaze | — | No mask. The mirror shows what the other masks have done |

**Masks as a psychological idea.** Goffman (1959) described everyday life as a performance in which people manage the impression they give each audience. Harter et al. (1996) studied **false self behavior** in adolescents: acting unlike your real self to win approval. Teens who did it because they felt they had to (rather than to experiment) reported lower self-worth and less sense of who they really were. That's exactly what the masks represent.

## 2. Room by room

### 2.1 Family Room

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Setting** | A Korean family apartment: a dining table, family photos on the wall, slippers at the door. In later loops, the holiday table with extended family appears |
| **Characters** [Proposed] | **Mother** and **Father** (love expressed as worry) · **Older sibling** (the one you're compared to) · **Aunt** (at the holiday table; says out loud what the parents only hint) |
| **Tactics** | Harm framed as care; comparison with the sibling; the graduation gift; holiday nagging |
| **Real-world basis** | Surgery as a graduation gift (concept doc); a Sep 2026 survey found sibling comparison was the most common holiday conversation topic (27.9%), and "excessive interference from relatives" the #1 custom people want changed (48.5%) |

| Loop | Example lines (daughter / son variants) |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | "We just want what's best for you." · "Your sister looked so fresh at her graduation. We booked you a consultation too, as a gift!" / "Stand up straight, you look short in photos." |
| 2 | "That's good, but what about…" · Aunt: "You've gained a little, haven't you? It's fine, it's fine." |
| 3 | Word-for-word repetition of Loop 1 lines, said while looking at the damage as if it isn't there |
| 4+ | "We never told you to do *this*." |

### 2.2 Friends & Partner Room

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Setting** | A café booth or a friend's room; the partner sits slightly apart, on their phone |
| **Characters** [Proposed] | **Best friend** (the "honest" one) · **Two friends** (the group chorus) · **Partner** (a girlfriend or boyfriend; gender set by the player) · an **ex**, only ever mentioned |
| **Tactics** | Cruelty as jokes ("I'm just being honest"); **fat talk** (friends criticizing their own bodies so you join in); comparison with the partner's ex and friends' partners; quiet withdrawal |
| **Real-world basis** | Fat talk is common among young women and raises body dissatisfaction whatever a person's actual size (Salk & Engeln-Maddox, 2011). For men, peer appearance talk predicted the drive for muscularity in Korean undergraduates (*IJERPH*, 2020) |

| Loop | Example lines |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | "Ugh, I'm so bloated today. You're lucky… well, kind of." · Partner: "You'd look so good if you just…" |
| 2 | "Didn't we already talk about your weight?" "We did?" "No, no, just messing with you! First time, I swear." (concept doc) |
| 3 | Friends laugh at the same joke, same timing, every visit |
| 4+ | They flinch when the protagonist sits down |

### 2.3 Society Room

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Setting** | Alternates between a part-time job (a café counter or convenience store) and an interview room with a panel and a photo on the résumé |
| **Characters** [Proposed] | **Manager** (customer-facing "image") · **Coworker** (the one who gets the better shifts) · **Interviewer** (reads the résumé photo before the résumé) |
| **Tactics** | Appearance framed as professionalism; being passed over; "it's not personal, it's the job" |
| **Real-world basis** | Lookism in hiring: in 2017 most Korean private employers still required résumé photos, and Korea banned them in public-sector hiring (blind hiring) |

| Loop | Example lines |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | "Customers like a clean, fresh look. Just a tip!" · Interviewer: "Nice photo. Is that recent?" |
| 2 | "Your coworker's getting the front counter this week. Nothing personal." |
| 3 | The résumé photo no longer matches the face in the room, and nobody mentions it |
| 4+ | The panel interviews the photo instead of the protagonist |

### 2.4 Social Media Room

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Setting** | The protagonist's room, but the walls are a phone screen. The room is played through an **in-game phone/feed UI** (PRD P1 #15) |
| **Characters** | No faces **[Decided]**: a feed, a chorus of comments, an influencer whose face is always the "after", and the algorithm, which speaks in notifications |
| **Tactics** | Infinite scroll; filters; before/afters; likes as currency; the clinic ads (subway ads and clinic-covered streets from the concept doc) |
| **Real-world basis** | Time on Facebook and appearance comparison there worsened young women's body image and mood (Fardouly et al., 2015). Surgeons report patients asking to look like their **filtered selfies** (Rajanala et al., 2018). Likes activate the brain's reward circuitry in teens (Sherman et al., 2016) |

| Loop | Example feed content |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Compliments and likes; a "glow-up" filter that feels like fun |
| 2 | "Not good enough" comments mixed in; clinic ads matching the player's last upgrade |
| 3 | The filter stops coming off |
| 4+ | Every post is the same post; the like count goes backwards |

### 2.5 Bathroom

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Setting** | A small tiled bathroom with one mirror and the rose in its glass case on the shelf |
| **What the player does** | Checks appearance and status (the mirror is the stats screen); rates themselves 0–10; later, talks to the Reflection |
| **Role in the game** | Self-checking hub; where the damage is faced; where the Neutral and Special endings happen ([endings](endings.md)) |
| **Real-world basis** | Mirror checking is a core body-dysmorphia behavior that keeps the worry going (Veale, 2004). Mirror *exposure* (looking calmly and describing without judgment) is a real treatment for body-image distress (Delinsky & Wilson, 2006). The Bathroom holds both: the trap and the way out |

## 3. Loudness

The quiz decides which room is loudest ([quiz design §4](act0-quiz.md)). **[Proposed]**

| | Quiet room | Normal room | Loud room |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Upgrade offers per visit | Loop 1: 1 · then 1 | Loop 1: 1 · then 1–2 | Loop 1: 1 · then 2–3 |
| Lines | Generic | Personal | Personal and uses the player's own quiz answers (e.g. the "sunny windowsill" player hears "We've always looked after you") |
| Presence in other rooms | None | None | Its voices leak into other rooms from Loop 3 |

---

## 4. Son and daughter variants

Every room needs both. Most lines change only a word; some pressures differ by gender in real life (thinness and facial features vs. height and muscle), but **any upgrade path can be offered to either protagonist** ([upgrades & reveals](upgrades-and-reveals.md)).
